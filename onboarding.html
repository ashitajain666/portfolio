01J.P. Morgan Private Bank · Intake Onboarding

Helping advisors catch onboarding errors before review.

I worked on an AI review step inside an advisor’s account-opening workflow. It compared the information entered in Intake with documents and other records, then surfaced discrepancies for the advisor to check.

The aim was to catch problems before the case reached quality control. The advisor still had to review the evidence and decide what to change. The design covered the review itself and what happened around it: waiting for results, checking a recommendation, replacing a document, handling a failure and continuing to the next stage.

02The problem

The problem was in the review queue.

The project started with slow account-opening reviews. The analysis in the deck pointed to repetitive checks and a queue made worse by understaffing.

A reviewer had to spot differences between the information entered in the application and the supporting records. A name could differ from an identity document. An address document could be too old. A utility bill could be missing a postcode.

These are different problems. Some need a corrected value. Others need a new document. The design had to make that difference clear.

The goal was to catch these issues earlier, without making the advisor treat an AI suggestion as a verified fact.

03Scope

What the review could and could not do.

The flow sat inside an existing account-opening process. It came after the advisor had entered account and party details and before Prefill Review.

The design checked several sources. The flow names demographic records, documents in DocSuite and conversation data in Connect. Those checks could produce different kinds of recommendations.

The advisor could accept some suggested changes in Intake. Other records had to be changed elsewhere. The flow directed email and phone changes to Profile360 rather than allowing them in Intake.

The review also had to work when the AI was slow or failed. The flow included a way to retry or continue to Prefill Review without the AI review.

04Process

I started with the output, not the assistant.

I looked at what the review would return before deciding how it should appear in the interface.

The output examples identified the party, the relevant section, the discrepancy and supporting detail. An expired identity document came with an expiry date. An incomplete address came with the missing part. An old address document came with its document date.

That gave the interface a clear job: show what was wrong, show what the system had compared and give the advisor an appropriate next action.

05Process

I explored an assistant and recommendations on the page.

An assistant could answer follow-up questions, change context and support several AI tasks in one place. It also had room for longer explanations.

But the comparison in the deck identified a problem: an extra window could cover the worklist or case details where the advisor needed to act.

An in-page approach kept the recommendation closer to the task. It could also leave content on the page for another user to see. Its limits were different: the content was preformatted, it did not support follow-up questions, and the layout needed to work whether a recommendation existed or not.

I explored both approaches. The detailed flow supplied here uses a review page with recommendations in the account-opening workflow.

06Process

I tried different ways to compare the values.

I explored a summary table, a choice between the existing and suggested values, and a card with the input beside the recommendation.

The summary table put several differences in one place. The radio-button version made the choice explicit. The side-by-side card kept the discrepancy and the two values together.

The testing slide records two preferred options. One made the source of the information visible, described the discrepancy clearly and made accepting or rejecting easier to understand. The other took fewer clicks and made the input and AI suggestion easy to compare.

That was useful feedback, but it did not establish one clear winner on its own.

07Process

A recommendation needed an action, not just a warning.

The detailed flow separates a suggested value from a document problem.

For a field discrepancy, the advisor sees the current input next to the recommendation. For an expired or incomplete document, the action is to upload a replacement.

For email and phone discrepancies, the page explains that the change cannot be accepted in Intake and directs the advisor to Profile360.

This matters because the same “Accept” action would not solve all three problems. A new value, a new document and a change in another system need different paths.

15Craft

The craft was in making a dense review readable.

The design had to sit inside a detailed account-opening page. Client context, account stages and party information were already present.

The review explorations use repeated comparison patterns rather than a different layout for each discrepancy. The current value, suggested value, explanation and action have distinct places. The detailed flow also groups recommendations by party and keeps an accepted count visible.

The document examples need more room than a simple field comparison. The design gives them a different action rather than forcing them into the same value-edit pattern.

16Testing

What testing told me.

The testing slide says users agreed that the AI use case could help address data inconsistencies in Intake 2.0.

Their feedback on the preferred options was specific. They wanted to see where the information came from, understand the discrepancy, compare their input with the suggestion and act without extra clicks.

That supports the direction of the review interface. It does not show that review time fell or that the model detected discrepancies accurately.

The detailed flow also includes a recommendation-rating prompt.
