The problem

Making online classes feel like a classroom

In 2020, MiDigi World asked us to build live online classes covering wellness, cooking, coding and more. We had weeks, not months. Courses cost ₹299 to ₹999 (about $4 to $12), and the teachers included chef Sanjeev Kapoor, footballer Bhaichung Bhutia, and actor Ashish Vidyarthi.

The whole point was learning from a real expert, not watching a video. Anyone can upload a video. The hard part was making it feel like a classroom, where learners could ask questions and get answers.

I talked to learners instead of guessing.

I interviewed eight learners over Zoom, from people who had never paid for online learning to frequent users. I followed up on WhatsApp, reached out through my own network, and reviewed competing products.

Three problems learners had with online classes

  • 1 No support after class.

    Learners could have questions after the class ended, but the teacher was not always available to help.

  • 2 Nobody checks their work.

    Learners could watch how to do something, but they did not get feedback on whether they did it correctly.

  • 3 No interaction between learners.

    Learners attended the same class but had few chances to talk, share ideas, or work together.

Design process · how I approached it

I treated this as a people problem. I asked questions like…

  • 1.What happens when learners have questions after class?
  • 2.How do learners know if they are doing something correctly?
  • 3.How can learners interact and learn from each other?
  • 4.Why might learners hesitate to ask questions during a live class?
Key design decisions

Three design decisions brought out the best of MiDigi World

1

Support after the class

Most questions came up after the session ended, and learners had no way to reach the expert who taught it. I explored different ways to extend support beyond the class before choosing one.

Exploration 1 of 3

Concept A: office hours

The expert stays on for thirty minutes after class, and anyone who joins can ask questions live.

Not chosenIt only helps learners who are free at that exact half hour, and every class costs the expert extra time.

Exploration 2 of 3

Concept B: ask the class

Learners post their question to everyone who attended. Other learners answer, and the instructor can add to it.

Not chosenSupport arrives, but not always from the expert, and wrong answers need moderation.

Exploration 3 of 3

Concept C: an answer within a day

The learner leaves a question and the expert replies when they are next available, within 24 hours.

Not chosenIt works at any hour, but there is no help in the moment the learner is stuck.

The chosen direction

A timed chat with the expert

Chat stays open for a set time after class, and the limit is visible from the start. The learner gets help right away. The expert gets a clear end.

ShippedImmediate help, direct from the expert, with a limit the business could afford. Small enough to build in eight weeks.

Final chat designs

Chat addressed all three problems: learners could reach the expert after class, share their work and get feedback, and stay connected with their classmates.

Chat was the core feature, supported by other features that made the learning experience more complete.

Reflection
What I would change

Test the chat from both sides.

I would push for lightweight usability testing before launch. Do learners understand the window before they send a question? Can experts quickly see what is open, what is ending, and what is closed?

“When you are the only designer in the room, your job is not to have every answer. It is to make the tradeoffs clear enough that the team can decide properly.”
